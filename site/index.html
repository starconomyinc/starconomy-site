01

Buyers are asked to trust a binary they cannot rebuild themselves.

A procurement team in Tokyo, Brussels, or Washington can read the datasheet and still not know whether the firmware is what the source says it is. A hash the vendor supplies only shows that a file did not change on the way. It does not show that the file was built from the audited source, with the declared toolchain, and with nothing else in it.

  1. 01

    No tampering

    Nothing is introduced between the source that was reviewed and the bytes that ship.

  2. 02

    No untrusted code

    Binary blobs, unpinned dependencies, and a toolchain only one party can run are not evidence.

  3. 03

    No single point of build trust

    A hash from the maker’s own CI is the maker grading their own work.

02

The bar for diligence is moving.

These dates are why buyers are asking. They are not certifications Starconomy holds. Binary Star is not a filing with a regulator. This page is not legal advice.

  1. December 2025

    The US FCC restricted foreign-produced unmanned aircraft systems and certain critical components.

  2. 2026

    ITRI became a Green UAS assessor.

  3. April 2026

    Taiwan revised its drone cybersecurity test specification.

  4. September 2026

    Vulnerability-reporting obligations under the EU Cyber Resilience Act began.

  5. December 2027

Makers who can hand a buyer a rebuild the buyer can check spend less of every sale defending a PDF.

03

How Binary Star works

Same source. Two rebuilds that cannot see each other. One comparison.

  1. 01

    Source

    The tree in scope, pinned dependencies, and a pinned toolchain. Both builds follow this, and not anything one side adds later.

  2. 02

    Two independent builds

    The release is rebuilt on separate runners, with no shared cache. The second build is there so the first one is not grading itself.

  3. 03

    Identical hashes

    The outputs are compared byte for byte, bootloader included. If they differ, the build does not pass. Close enough is not a result.

  4. 04

    Signed report

    The buyer receives a report in English, Japanese, and Traditional Chinese: the matching hashes, the SBOM, the signatures, and the build provenance.

04

What the buyer receives

These are evidence of a rebuild. They are not a government certificate, and they are not a finding that a product is legally compliant.

  1. 01

    Byte-for-byte rebuild

    Two independent builds, one hash — or the build does not pass.

  2. 02

    SBOM

    A software bill of materials, delivered as SPDX and as CycloneDX.

  3. 03

    Signed release

    The artifact the hash names, with a signature a buyer can check.

  4. 04

    Build provenance

    Recorded as in-toto statements, aligned with SLSA.

  5. 05

    Trilingual report

    English, Japanese, and Traditional Chinese, written so a buyer can check it without a call.

  6. 06

    The next release

    On retainer, every new release is verified again. A pass on one version is not reused for the next.

05

How to engage

We publish the scope, not a rate card. The fee follows the tree, the toolchain, and the buyer’s deadline.

  1. 01

    Readiness Scan

    A bounded look at whether this firmware can be rebuilt at all: toolchain, binary blobs, sources of non-determinism, and the claims a buyer would be unable to check today. The right first step when a sale depends on a question you have not answered yet.

  2. 02

    Build Assurance Audit

    The full Binary Star engagement. Source in an agreed scope, two independent rebuilds, an SBOM, a signed release, provenance, and the trilingual buyer report.

  3. 03

    Release Retainer

    Each later release verified again, by the same method. For teams that ship faster than a one-time report can keep up with.

  4. 04

    Supplier Verification

    For buyers in Japan

    An independent check of a supplier’s firmware claim, for a buyer in Japan who does not want the decision to rest on the supplier’s own build log.
